Research

Why do some animals regenerate while we scar?

Spiny mice regrow skin, hair follicles, muscle, and nerve. Salamanders regrow limbs. Laboratory mice and humans seal the wound with scar and call it done. That contrast is our experiment: by asking what regenerative animals do differently, we are working out how injured and transplanted organs could be repaired so well that they simply work again.

We hold regeneration to a functional standard. Tissue that regrows must also feel, move, and do its job — so we follow recovery from the gene to the behavior of the whole animal, and we look for mechanisms that recur across tissues and species, because those are the ones most likely to reach patients.

01

Where the work is headed

Better repair and transplantation of organs, through three connected goals.

Goal 01Innervation

Higher-quality innervation of repaired and transplanted organs.

Every transplanted organ arrives without its nerves. Surgery reconnects the blood supply; the nervous system does not follow. A transplanted heart beats without autonomic control, a kidney filters without the feedback that calibrates it, and even after injuries short of transplant, regrowing axons rarely find their original targets precisely enough to restore full function.

Spiny mice regrow nerves into brand-new whisker follicles with targeted, sheathed innervation. We study how that targeting works, what supports it, and what common mammals lack.

What we measure

Nerve regrowth into defined targets · Schwann cells and myelination · Reconnection with the brain (barrel cortex)

Goal 02Fibrosis

Healing that doesn't end in scar.

Scar closes a wound fast, but it is not the tissue that was lost: it grows no hair, houses no sensory end-organs, and is poorly innervated. In most mammals, including us, fibrosis is the default. Spiny mice take a different path after the same injury — they rebuild.

With transcriptomics and AI-assisted analysis we are looking for the decision point after injury where a tissue commits to rebuilding or to scarring, and the signals that push it one way or the other. The goal is not a gene list but a lever.

What we measure

Regeneration vs. scarring gene programs (Acomys vs. Mus) · Fibroblast and immune cell behavior · Tissue architecture after healing

Goal 03Functional recovery

Evidence that the organ actually works.

Regrowing tissue is not the same as restoring function, and a good behavioral score can hide the difference: an animal may have reinnervated its targets, or it may have learned to compensate without them. Those are not the same achievement, and they do not mean the same thing for therapy.

So we test whether structure predicts function — whether nerves regrew into the right places, whether those places reconnected with the brain, and whether the behavior that depends on that circuit actually came back.

What we measure

Sensation, movement, learning and memory · Structure–function alignment or dissociation · Baselines in uninjured animals

02

How we work

A mechanism is only visible against a contrast, so we compare animals that succeed with close relatives that fail, at every level of biology.

Spiny mouse · Acomys

A mammal that regenerates.

Regrows skin, whisker follicles, muscle, cartilage, and nerve after wounding, and is close enough to laboratory mice to compare gene by gene. Our whisker-pad model gives each follicle a defined nerve target, so we can ask precisely whether regrowing nerves found the right place.

Salamanders

The benchmark for regeneration.

Salamanders rebuild entire limbs. We are characterizing olfactory and tactile sensation across species as the baseline for asking how an animal's behavior during recovery shapes the quality of its reinnervation.

Laboratory mouse · Mus

The control that scars.

Same injury, different outcome. Comparing Mus with Acomys is how a mechanism specific to one tissue is told apart from one fundamental to regeneration — and from what humans are missing.

Every level of organization

Genes→ Cells→ Tissues→ Neural circuits→ Behavior→ Translation→ Human organ repair

A gene program is meaningless if the tissue never works; a behavioral recovery is uninterpretable without knowing what the cells did. So we work the whole chain, from transcript to patient, using histology, immunohistochemistry, behavioral assays, electrophysiology, genomics, and bioinformatics.

03

Current projects

Innervation

The whisker pad as a model for functional reinnervation.

Each whisker follicle is a discrete sensory end-organ with defined innervation targets, which makes it possible to ask exactly whether regenerating nerves find the right targets and restore the right signals. We use this system to identify the molecular, cellular, and behavioral conditions that support high-quality reinnervation at the organ level.

Functional recovery

Does structure predict function?

We quantify structural recovery at the level of individual nerves, end-organs, and skin architecture, then test whether that structure predicts behavior. Alignment means reinnervation succeeded; dissociation means compensation is doing the work. Variability in these outcomes is not noise — it is the signal.

Innervation · Behavior

Behavior as a driver of reinnervation.

We hypothesize that sensory feedback and behavioral engagement during recovery are not consequences of reinnervation but drivers of it. Finding the windows when behavior most influences nerve guidance is how we design rehabilitation that works alongside molecular and surgical therapies, not after them.

Functional recovery

Memory and cognition in spiny mice.

You cannot measure restoration without knowing what you are restoring. Spiny mice show enhanced learning and memory relative to laboratory mice; we are characterizing that cognitive baseline before asking how injury and reinnervation change it. Supported by a KSU PrePI grant, with Dr. Vishnu Suppiramaniam and Dr. Erica Holliday.

Functional recovery

Olfactory and tactile sensation in salamanders.

Before asking whether a salamander recovers its senses after injury, we need to know what those senses are. We are characterizing olfaction and touch across salamander species as the baseline for future studies of functional reinnervation. Supported by a KSU Mentor Protégé grant, with Dr. Todd Pierson.

Fibrosis

Decoding regeneration versus scarring with genomics and AI.

Transcriptomics, bioinformatics, and AI-assisted analysis to reveal the genes and cell populations that drive regeneration in spiny mice and to locate where the scarring response diverges. A mechanism that recurs across skin, ear, and nerve, and across species, is a candidate for therapy; conservation is our filter.
